## Reader assessment — descending order of impact

This is reviewer commentary, not authored essay text. None of the following
supplies replacement prose or has been inserted into the manuscript.

The essays read as a personal manifesto wrapped around a useful explanatory
model. The pyramid, post-its and Pixar console give the argument a memorable
physical shape; the voice is recognizable and often funny. The strongest passages
let the reader perform the inference themselves. I lose confidence when that
careful explanatory mode abruptly becomes certainty about neuroscience,
alignment or inevitable extinction. The energy is effective, but the argument
currently asks that energy to bridge some missing steps.

1. **Anti-capture argument — Core values, “this signal is WEAK AS FUCK” and the
   following paragraph.** This is the largest logical gap. Weak individual
   evidence does not establish that an optimizer must represent everyone, weight
   everyone fairly, or decline to serve one principal. Learning from many people
   and optimizing for many people's welfare are different requirements. The
   paragraph needs the missing mechanism and a way it could fail. The claim that
   a mistaken request necessarily causes immediate extinction increases the gap
   rather than resolving it.
2. **Recovery of wants — Core values, “The attention stream has the data” through
   “So now we know how to extract the preference signal.”** The passage moves from
   a plausible source of evidence to identification of wants without showing how
   competing explanations are separated. Attention, cost, constraints, mistakes,
   unknown alternatives and a reader's own prediction errors can produce similar
   observations. The engineer/actor examples illustrate the model; they do not
   demonstrate that the proposed measurements recover the right explanation.
   This matters especially because the current engine keeps those estimates
   unadmitted or unknown.
3. **Status of claims — across all three essays.** Definitions, metaphors,
   observations, hypotheses and necessity claims often have the same grammatical
   certainty. Especially check “absolutely a necessary first step,” “Affective
   empathy is basically just mirror neurons,” “no nerve cells are firing together,”
   and the transition from a predictive model to a target for aligned behavior.
   A reader needs to know what you stipulate, what you propose, and what the cited
   evidence actually establishes. This is about marking the boundaries of the
   argument, not removing your voice.
4. **Human skill erosion and model collapse — Expertise, “AI mucks this whole
   entire process up.”** The analogy becomes a claim of the same causal mechanism
   without an intervening argument. The paragraph also spans interpretability,
   RLHF, detecting generated work, learning noise and skill loss very quickly.
   Distinguish which links are demonstrated and which are your proposed account.
   The nearby doctor's caption generalizes a specific detection-rate outcome to
   “accuracy”; its skill-decay comparison remains an explicitly uncited personal
   assertion. That breadth affects credibility more than caption polish.
5. **Emotions, drives and Barrett — Core values, the Inside Out sequence.** The
   earlier essay describes emotions as inherited and largely fixed; this essay
   later makes emotions learned and constructed. You acknowledge imprecision,
   but the reader still has to undo a previously taught model. “But she's wrong”
   then changing the definition leaves it unclear whether the disagreement is
   empirical or terminological. Identify the exact point of disagreement and
   keep the roles of drive, emotion, need and value trackable.
6. **The first essay's promise and emotional burden — Current attention.** A
   reader clicks for an explanation of a display and receives moral hazard,
   abuse and extinction before the operational explanation arrives. Your final
   acknowledgment is funny, but does not recover readers who left earlier. In the
   closing essay, repeated inevitable-death statements can feel coercive and
   crowd out the hopeful collective invitation. Decide how much of that burden
   this particular entry point should carry.
7. **Confidence versus correctness — Expertise, “near 100% confidence.”** The
   next paragraph rightly explores missing context and mistaken inference. The
   earlier formulation can make expertise sound like near-certainty of being
   right rather than a confident prediction that may be wrong. That ambiguity
   directly affects how readers interpret the tool's graphs and ratings.
8. **Local pacing and syntax — end of Expertise and opening caption.** The SIFT
   passage repeats the temporary-solution argument in adjacent sentences. “trying
   something to make something” and “right now to not need to manually” obscure
   the intended action; the latter's stacked negation makes the conclusion hard
   to parse. Moving “in my free time” into the first caption's time-and-effort
   phrase also interrupts its grammar. These are smaller than the argument gaps.
9. **Mechanical errata — lowest impact.** Check “repesent” in the pyramid
   paragraph, “For the an early look” in the next paragraph, “accellerates” in the
   AI paragraph, and “Thought I guess” in the spaceship caption. The first and
   final “current attention” quotation marks also produce punctuation worth a
   deliberate style check. These remain exactly as authored or dictated.

My priority would be the first two arguments, then the distinction between
proposed and established claims. Those changes would make the work more persuasive
without requiring a less personal voice. This is a reading and reasoning pass,
not a comprehensive audit of every citation.
